The Zen Journey — A Quiet Passage Through Three Kamakura Temples

Seated meditation and a bowl of matcha, across a half or full day

Kamakura has carried the quiet discipline of Zen for eight centuries, but reaching that stillness depends on when and where you walk. Across one day, this route moves through three of Kamakura's Five Great Zen Temples — Engaku-ji, Jochi-ji, and Jomyo-ji — pausing for seated meditation and a bowl of matcha along the way.

01

01｜Engaku-ji — The Seated Meditation
Engaku-ji, the approach through its main gate
Founded by Hōjō Tokimune, Engaku-ji remains the head temple of its own Zen lineage, where practice continues today. In the meditation hall at the rear of the grounds, guests take a quiet seat for zazen, guided throughout by an English-speaking guide. Timed around the temple's quieter hours, the stillness here comes closer to the real thing.


02｜Jochi-ji — The Moss-Covered Approach
Jochi-ji, its moss-covered stone steps
A short walk from Engaku-ji, Jochi-ji's moss-covered steps come into view. Ranked fourth among Kamakura's Five Great Zen Temples yet rarely crowded, its tree-shaded grounds are remarkably quiet. Simply climbing and descending the steps, unhurried, reveals the kind of spaciousness that defines Kamakura's Zen temples.

03｜Lunch — A Meal Suited to You
Shōjin cuisine, a seasonal dish laid out on the table

After Jochi-ji, the day pauses for lunch at a Kamakura restaurant chosen to suit each guest's taste — from traditional shōjin (Buddhist vegetarian) cuisine to a dish built around the season's ingredients. Unhurried, it becomes less an interruption than a chance to sit with the morning's quiet a little longer.

04｜Jomyo-ji — Matcha Over the Dry Garden
Jomyo-ji, its dry-landscape garden and tea room

After lunch, the day closes at Jomyo-ji, where a tea room looks out over a carefully raked dry-landscape garden. Seated before it with a bowl of matcha, there's little to do but let the pace of the afternoon settle in — perhaps the closest the day comes to Zen itself.

What the tour handles for you

From the etiquette of seated meditation to temple admissions, matcha, lunch, and transfers within Kamakura and Fujisawa, an English-speaking guide handles it all. Available as a half-day (approx. 4 hours) or full-day (approx. 8 hours) journey, with the full day allowing more time to sit with each temple. It can also be paired with other cultural experiences — ikebana or swordsmithing among them — for an even more considered day.
